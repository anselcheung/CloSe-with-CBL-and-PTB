\begin{abstract}
Semantic segmentation of 3D point clouds is a fundamental task in computer vision that benefits from directly exploiting geometric information to produce view-consistent point-wise predictions. However, many point cloud segmentation networks struggle to accurately delineate object boundaries, reducing segmentation quality around object edges. Recent methods such as Push-the-Boundary (PTB) and Contrastive Boundary Learning (CBL) address this through boundary-aware supervised learning and feature propagation. In this work, we integrate PTB, CBL, and a boundary-aware refinement module into CloSe-Net, and further adapt a SegFix-inspired post-processing strategy from image segmentation to the 3D point cloud domain. We ablate all component combinations on CloSe-D and Thuman2.0 over three seeds, using boundary- and region-level metrics, to assess how well existing boundary refinement strategies transfer to CloSe-Net.

Across 60 trained models (10 configurations $\times$ 3 seeds $\times$ 2
codebook-init regimes), no configuration detectably improves aggregate B-IoU
over its own baseline: the plain baseline is in fact the best non-CLIP B-IoU
in the entire campaign. The signal these methods do produce is not invisible,
just not where the aggregate metric looks -- it shows up per class (largest
on Dress and Jumpsuits under PTB+BRM+CBL, though within seed spread for at
least one of them) and in a narrow, specific CLIP interaction (CLIP-init
detectably damages the plain baseline's B-IoU and freq\_IoU, and our methods
neutralise but do not reverse that damage). We report all of this, including
the null results, at the same statistical standard throughout.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Semantic segmentation of 3D point clouds yields view-consistent, point-wise
predictions grounded directly in geometry, avoiding the multi-view
inconsistency of image-based approaches. Its persistent weakness is boundary
quality: local neighbourhood operations aggregate features from both sides of
a class transition, so representations blur exactly where class identity
changes. The effect is measurable on S3DIS~\cite{https://doi.org/10.57761/gk3g-wc33},
where accuracy restricted to boundary regions lags interior accuracy by around
twenty mIoU points~\cite{tang2022contrastive}, and it is disproportionately
costly for 3D clothing segmentation, where garment labels are defined almost
entirely by their extents: where a sleeve ends and the arm begins, where a hem
separates a shirt from trousers. Interior points are rarely ambiguous, so
aggregate metrics are dominated by regions that are already easy, while
downstream tasks such as garment extraction inherit every boundary error
directly.

Clothing segmentation is a setting where a domain-specific model is already
available. CloSe-Net~\cite{antic2024close} fuses a DGCNN~\cite{wang2019dynamicgraphcnnlearning}
point encoder, a canonical-pose body prior derived from a fitted
SMPL~\cite{SMPL:2015} body, and a learnable garment codebook attended over by
a cross-attention module, into a pointwise MLP decoder (\cref{sec:methodology}
gives the full architecture). It attains strong region-level accuracy on
CloSe-D, but on out-of-distribution scans such as THuman2.0~\cite{tao2021function4d}
the original authors report blurry boundaries, addressed through interactive
annotation and continual fine-tuning rather than through the model itself.
This leaves open whether an architectural boundary mechanism could serve the
same purpose, and whether a purely semantic route -- sharpening the garment
codebook itself rather than the geometry around it -- could serve a
complementary one. Several boundary mechanisms have been proposed for point
clouds generally, and a semantic prior is available off-the-shelf: this paper
adapts both to CloSe-Net and asks how well each transfers.

Push-the-Boundary (PTB)~\cite{Du_2022} adds auxiliary boundary-classification
and interior-direction streams, then fuses both to guide feature propagation
during decoder upsampling. Contrastive Boundary Learning
(CBL)~\cite{tang2022contrastive} applies an InfoNCE objective at boundary
points to separate differing-class neighbours in feature space, at every
sub-sampling stage of the backbone. SegFix~\cite{yuan2020segfixmodelagnosticboundaryrefinement}
instead post-processes, replacing unreliable boundary predictions with labels
propagated from confident interior points; it is model-agnostic, though to our
knowledge it has not previously been adapted to 3D point clouds.

Each of these was developed and validated on generic indoor and outdoor
benchmarks such as S3DIS and ScanNet~\cite{dai2017scannet}, and two
differences suggest that their transfer to clothed human scans is not
automatic. The first is geometric: in generic scenes, class boundaries tend to
coincide with structure, whereas a hem lies flush against the torso and a cuff
against an arm -- the class changes while the surface stays smooth. Methods
that localise boundaries from geometric cues may therefore have less signal to
exploit; Du et al.\ attribute PTB's largest per-class regression to a category
with geometrically irregular boundaries, pointing to noisy boundary and
direction estimates as the cause.

The second difference is architectural, and it is the one we focus on. Both
PTB and CBL are formulated for backbones with a sub-sampling hierarchy. PTB's
guided propagation reweights the interpolation performed when upsampling
features, steering an aggregation that already exists and that PTB identifies
as a source of lost boundary detail; CBL's own ablation attributes most of its
improvement to applying the contrastive objective across sub-sampling stages
rather than at the input scale alone. CloSe-Net provides neither: its DGCNN
encoder runs at full point resolution and its MLP decoder performs no
neighbourhood aggregation at all. Porting PTB therefore requires re-homing its
guided-propagation mechanism rather than transplanting it directly -- there is
no upsampling stage left to guide -- and whether either method retains its
intended effect once that precondition is removed is, to our knowledge,
untested.

A semantic route to the same goal is also available and, to our knowledge,
unexplored for this architecture: CloSe-Net's garment codebook is a randomly
initialised embedding table trained from scratch, with no prior over which
garment classes are visually or semantically related. Seeding it instead from
a frozen vision-language model's text embeddings~\cite{radford2021clip} gives
semantically close classes (a dress and a jumpsuit, say) nearby codebook
vectors before training even begins, which may ease exactly the boundaries
where the garment-attention module has to disambiguate between similar
classes -- a different mechanism from PTB/CBL/SegFix's geometric boundary
supervision, and one we evaluate independently and in combination with it.

We investigate both routes directly. We integrate PTB's auxiliary supervision
and CBL's contrastive objective into CloSe-Net; implement a Boundary-aware
Refinement Module (BRM) that re-homes PTB's guided-propagation weighting onto
a fixed-neighbourhood aggregation of pre-logit decoder features, since
CloSe-Net has no upsampling stage of its own; adapt SegFix to the point cloud
setting; and initialise the garment codebook from CLIP~\cite{radford2021clip}
text embeddings of the class names. We evaluate every combination on CloSe-D
augmented with THuman2.0 across three seeds, using boundary- and region-level
metrics~\cite{tang2022contrastive,biou}, and report only effects that exceed
seed variance. Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item A unified integration of three boundary-aware strategies into
        CloSe-Net -- PTB-style auxiliary supervision, a refinement module
        (BRM) adapting PTB's guided feature propagation to a backbone without
        an upsampling hierarchy, and CBL's contrastive objective, including
        the feature stage it is applied at.
  \item A point cloud adaptation of SegFix-style boundary post-processing.
  \item A CLIP-initialised garment codebook, a semantic-prior alternative to
        the geometric boundary mechanisms above, evaluated both independently
        and in combination with them.
  \item A THuman2.0-derived augmentation of the CloSe-D training split.
  \item A multi-seed ablation over all component combinations, under both a
        randomly initialised and a CLIP-initialised codebook.
  \item An analysis of when these strategies transfer to 3D clothing
        segmentation, separating geometric from architectural causes and
        identifying the evaluation choices that determine whether a boundary
        method appears to help.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{3D point cloud and clothing segmentation.} Point-based backbones such
as DGCNN~\cite{wang2019dynamicgraphcnnlearning} build a $k$-nearest-neighbour
graph in feature space and aggregate it via stacked EdgeConv blocks, without
requiring the sub-sampling hierarchy that voxel- or hierarchy-based backbones
use. CloSe-Net~\cite{antic2024close} builds directly on this to segment
clothing on human scans by fusing a DGCNN point encoder with a body-shape
prior and a learnable per-garment codebook, and is the base architecture we
extend throughout this paper. It follows a line of work on parsing 3D
clothing, including SIZER~\cite{tiwari20sizer}, which paired a clothing
segmentation model with size-sensitive garment reconstruction; unlike that
line of work, our focus is specifically on boundary quality rather than on
downstream garment reconstruction or size estimation.

\textbf{Boundary-aware segmentation.} PTB~\cite{Du_2022}, CBL~\cite{tang2022contrastive},
and SegFix~\cite{yuan2020segfixmodelagnosticboundaryrefinement} represent three
different mechanisms for the same underlying problem: PTB adds auxiliary
supervision that guides an existing feature-propagation step, CBL reshapes the
feature space directly with a contrastive objective at every backbone stage,
and SegFix relabels low-confidence predictions at test time without touching
training at all. All three were developed and validated on generic indoor and
outdoor scene benchmarks such as S3DIS~\cite{https://doi.org/10.57761/gk3g-wc33}
and ScanNet~\cite{dai2017scannet}, not on clothed human scans, and (with the
exception of CBL, whose ablation already probes a limited-hierarchy setting)
not on backbones lacking a sub-sampling hierarchy -- the gap this paper's
architectural analysis addresses.

\textbf{Semantic priors from vision-language models.} CLIP~\cite{radford2021clip}
learns a joint image-text embedding space from web-scale image-caption pairs,
and its frozen text encoder is widely used as an off-the-shelf source of
semantic structure over a fixed label set in other tasks. We use it in this
role: as a one-time initialisation for CloSe-Net's garment codebook, rather
than as a jointly-trained multimodal component, so it composes with the
purely geometric boundary mechanisms above without architectural changes.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{CloSe-Net backbone}
\label{sec:methodology:backbone}

CloSe-Net~\cite{antic2024close} segments a coloured scan point cloud into 18
clothing classes from three fused per-point feature sources. A
DGCNN~\cite{wang2019dynamicgraphcnnlearning} point encoder takes points (xyz,
colour, normals) and applies three stacked EdgeConv blocks over a
$k$-nearest-neighbour graph in feature space ($k{=}40$), each block
max-pooled over its neighbourhood; the three blocks' outputs are concatenated
(192-d) alongside a 1024-d feature pooled globally over the whole point cloud.
A canonical-pose body encoder passes through each point's coordinate in the
SMPL~\cite{SMPL:2015} canonical (unposed) frame unchanged (3-d), giving the
network a body-shape prior independent of pose. A garment encoder attends
over a learnable codebook: queries are the DGCNN's per-point features, keys
and values are a $18\times64$ embedding table (one row per garment class),
masked so that only classes actually present in the scan can be attended to,
via a standard multi-head-attention $+$ feed-forward block. The three
resulting per-point features are concatenated (1283-d) and passed through a
shared pointwise MLP decoder -- a stack of $1{\times}1$ convolutions
($1283\rightarrow512\rightarrow512\rightarrow512\rightarrow512\rightarrow256\rightarrow18$,
each followed by batch norm, LeakyReLU, and dropout except the last) -- to
produce per-point class logits. Training uses cross-entropy on a 2048-point
per-scan subsample.

\subsection{PTB-style auxiliary boundary supervision}
\label{sec:methodology:ptb}

Following PTB~\cite{Du_2022}, we attach two auxiliary heads alongside the
segmentation decoder, both consuming the same 1283-d fused feature: a
boundary head predicting whether a point lies on a semantic boundary, and a
direction head regressing a unit vector from the nearest boundary into the
object's interior. Both are pointwise MLPs of the same shape as the
segmentation decoder's early layers ($1283\rightarrow256\rightarrow128\rightarrow\{2,3\}$),
with the direction head's output $\ell_2$-normalised. The boundary head is
trained with a class-weighted cross-entropy (boundary-class weight
$\beta{=}0.6$) and the direction head with an $\ell_2$ loss against unit
ground-truth vectors, added to the segmentation loss as
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{segm}} + \lambda_B \mathcal{L}_{\text{boundary}} + \lambda_D \mathcal{L}_{\text{direction}},
  \label{eq:ptb-loss}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_B{=}3.0$, $\lambda_D{=}0.3$. Ground-truth boundary membership
and direction are precomputed once per scan, at full mesh resolution (before
the 2048-point training subsample, so the boundary set does not depend on
which points happen to be sampled): a point is a boundary point if any of its
$k{=}4$ nearest neighbours carries a different ground-truth label, and its
direction target is the normalised vector from the nearest such boundary
point.

\subsection{Boundary-aware Refinement Module (BRM)}
\label{sec:methodology:brm}

PTB's own boundary mechanism -- guided feature propagation -- reweights the
interpolation used when a backbone upsamples features from a sub-sampled
bottleneck back to full resolution. CloSe-Net has no such stage
(\cref{sec:intro}), so instead of reweighting an interpolation, we reweight a
spatial neighbourhood aggregation inserted between the decoder's pre-logit
(256-d) features and its final classification layer. For each point $i$, we
gather its $k{=}6$ spatial nearest neighbours and aggregate their pre-logit
features with weight
\begin{equation}
  w_{ij} = \Big[\, \exp\!\big(-d_{ij}/r\big) \;+\; \alpha \cdot \exp\!\big(\hat{P}_{b,i} - 1\big) \cdot \cos\theta_{ij} \,\Big]_{+},
  \label{eq:brm-weight}
\end{equation}
normalised to sum to 1 over the $k$ neighbours, where $d_{ij}$ is the
Euclidean distance to neighbour $j$, $\hat P_{b,i}$ is point $i$'s predicted
boundary probability (from \cref{sec:methodology:ptb}), and $\theta_{ij}$ is
the angle between the neighbour offset and point $i$'s predicted interior
direction. $r$ is a per-module scale, initialised at $0.05$ and left
\emph{learnable} rather than fixed at PTB's original room-scale value
($r{=}0.125$, tuned for metre-scale S3DIS rooms rather than unit-bounding-box
human scans), and $\alpha{=}1.0$. Interior points ($\hat P_{b,i}\approx0$)
degrade gracefully to plain distance-weighted smoothing. The aggregated
neighbourhood feature is passed through a small residual block (a
$1{\times}1$ convolution, batch norm, and LeakyReLU) and added back to the
point's own pre-logit feature; the refined feature is then passed through the
\emph{same trained} final classification layer used by the plain decoder,
rather than a new one, so BRM only ever changes what feeds an already-trained
classifier.

\subsection{SegFix-style post-processing}
\label{sec:methodology:segfix}

As a zero-training-cost complement to the two mechanisms above, we adapt
SegFix~\cite{yuan2020segfixmodelagnosticboundaryrefinement}'s test-time
relabeling to point clouds. For every point whose predicted boundary
probability exceeds a threshold ($0.7$), we walk a virtual position along its
predicted interior direction in fixed steps (by default, twice the scan's
median nearest-neighbour spacing) and, at each of $n$ steps, re-query a
$k$-d tree built over the scan for the nearest point and copy its predicted
label. Re-querying against the \emph{advancing} position at every step, rather
than a single precomputed target, lets a chain of adjacent boundary points
hand a confident interior label down the line over several hops; we use
$n{=}2$ throughout. This step requires no retraining and is applied purely at
evaluation time on top of any of the trained variants above.

\subsection{Contrastive Boundary Learning (CBL)}
\label{sec:methodology:cbl}

Following CBL~\cite{tang2022contrastive}, we add a supervised contrastive loss
that shapes a chosen feature representation directly, independent of the
auxiliary heads above. For a $k$-nearest-neighbour ($k{=}40$, capped to
radius $0.1$) neighbourhood of $\ell_2$-normalised features, a point is an
\emph{anchor} if its neighbourhood contains both same-label and
different-label points under the ground-truth segmentation (no separate
positive/negative sampler is needed). The loss pulls each anchor's
same-label neighbours together and pushes its full neighbourhood apart in a
supervised InfoNCE form,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{CBL}} = -\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}}
  \log \frac{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i),\, y_j = y_i} \exp\!\big(-d_{\cos}(i,j)/\tau\big)}
             {\sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} \exp\!\big(-d_{\cos}(i,j)/\tau\big)},
  \label{eq:cbl-loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{A}$ is the anchor set, $\mathcal{N}(i)$ point $i$'s
neighbourhood, $d_{\cos}$ cosine distance, and $\tau{=}1.0$ a temperature.
Unlike the geometric mechanisms above, CBL can be computed at any feature
stage; our implementation supports this as a configurable choice (the
decoder's pre-logit feature, the DGCNN encoder's third EdgeConv stage, or all
three EdgeConv stages summed, mirroring the multi-scale form of CBL's own
Eq.~7), but the campaign in this report uses only the paper's own default --
the decoder's pre-logit feature, weighted at $0.1$. We did not carry a
seeded, paired ablation of the other two feature stages through this
campaign, so we do not report a comparison between them here.

\subsection{CLIP-initialised garment codebook}
\label{sec:methodology:clip}

As a semantic, rather than geometric, route to the same goal
(\cref{sec:intro}), we replace the garment codebook's random initialisation
with one derived from a frozen CLIP~\cite{radford2021clip} text encoder
(ViT-B/32). For each of the 18 garment classes we encode the prompt
``a photo of \{classname\}'' to a 512-d text embedding, then project all 18
embeddings to the codebook's 64 dimensions with a single fixed, seeded random
Gaussian projection (a Johnson-Lindenstrauss-style projection rather than
PCA, since 18 samples admit at most 17 nonzero principal components). The
resulting $18\times64$ matrix initialises the garment codebook's embedding
table; the codebook is left trainable during CloSe-Net training (freezing it
is supported but not used for our primary results), so this only changes the
starting point the codebook is optimised from, not the optimisation itself.

\subsection{Dataset augmentation with THuman2.0}
\label{sec:methodology:thuman}

To increase training data volume, particularly for the boundary-supervised
variants above, we augment CloSe-D with THuman2.0~\cite{tao2021function4d}
scans converted into CloSe-D's own per-scan format. This required obtaining
THuman2.0's raw scans, its released SMPL (not SMPL-X) body fits, and the SMPL
neutral body model itself, and running the same preprocessing pipeline
CloSe-D's own scans go through (canonical-pose computation, colour and normal
extraction) on each THuman2.0 scan. Of THuman2.0's 526 scans, 500 have
usable garment labels; we combine these with CloSe-D's original split into a
single fixed-seed 80/10/10 train/val/test split, used for every ablation in
this paper. This step is purely infrastructural and makes no methodological
claim of its own. We did not separately evaluate a CloSe-D-only baseline, so
this report cannot isolate whether the THuman2.0 augmentation itself helped
or hurt any aggregate metric -- every number in this paper already includes
it.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

We evaluate whether the four mechanisms introduced in \cref{sec:methodology}
(PTB-style auxiliary supervision, BRM, SegFix-style post-processing, CBL) close
CloSe-Net's boundary-vs-interior gap (\cref{sec:intro}), and whether the
CLIP-initialised codebook offers an independent or complementary route to the
same goal. All experiments use CloSe-Di (18 garment classes) augmented with
THuman2.0 as described in \cref{sec:methodology:thuman}.

\textbf{Ablation grid.} PTB and CBL are each independently on or off; when PTB
is on, BRM may additionally be on (BRM requires PTB's auxiliary heads);
SegFix-style post-processing is an eval-only step applied on top of any
already-trained PTB-family checkpoint, so it is also independently on or off.
This yields the ten configurations listed as the row labels of
\cref{tab:headline}, from the plain baseline through every combination up to
PTB+BRM+CBL+SegFix. Each configuration is trained under 3 random seeds and, since
the garment codebook can be initialised either randomly or from CLIP text
embeddings (\cref{sec:methodology:clip}), evaluated under both regimes --
$10 \text{ configurations} \times 3 \text{ seeds} \times 2 \text{ init regimes} = 60$
trained models in total, whose paired results make up \cref{tab:headline}.

\textbf{Metrics.} All metrics are computed per test scan and averaged over the
test set. Let a scan have $N$ points with ground-truth labels $y_i$ and
predicted labels $\hat y_i$, $i=1,\dots,N$, over $C{=}18$ classes, and write
$P_c = \{i : \hat y_i = c\}$, $G_c = \{i : y_i = c\}$ for the predicted and
ground-truth point sets of class $c$.

\emph{mIoU.} The standard per-class intersection-over-union, averaged over
classes,
\begin{equation}
  \text{IoU}_c = \frac{|P_c \cap G_c|}{|P_c \cup G_c|}, \qquad
  \text{mIoU} = \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C} \text{IoU}_c,
  \label{eq:miou}
\end{equation}
where a class absent from both prediction and ground truth in a scan
($P_c = G_c = \varnothing$) is scored $\text{IoU}_c{=}1$ rather than
$0/0$, so it does not penalise that scan.

\emph{freq\_IoU.} The same per-class $\text{IoU}_c$, weighted by each class's
share of ground-truth points in the scan instead of averaged uniformly,
\begin{equation}
  \text{freq\_IoU} = \frac{\sum_{c=1}^{C} |G_c| \cdot \text{IoU}_c}{\sum_{c=1}^{C} |G_c|} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{c=1}^{C} |G_c| \cdot \text{IoU}_c,
  \label{eq:freqiou}
\end{equation}
so common classes (e.g.\ body, pants) dominate the score more than rare ones
(e.g.\ scarf), unlike mIoU where every class counts equally.

\emph{B-IoU.} Adapting the Boundary IoU protocol~\cite{biou} (developed for
image segmentation contours) to point sets, define the boundary set induced
by a labelling $z$ as
\begin{equation}
  B(z) = \big\{\, i \;:\; \exists\, j \in \mathcal{N}_k(i),\ z_j \neq z_i \,\big\},
  \label{eq:boundary-set}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ points whose $k$-nearest-neighbour set ($k{=}40$, capped to radius
$0.1$) contains a differently labelled point. B-IoU is then the set-overlap
IoU between the \emph{predicted} and \emph{ground-truth} boundary sets --
whether the model agrees with the ground truth on \emph{where} the
boundaries are -- rather than a class IoU:
\begin{equation}
  \text{B-IoU} = \frac{|B(\hat y) \cap B(y)|}{|B(\hat y) \cup B(y)|}.
  \label{eq:biou}
\end{equation}

\emph{mIoU@boundary / mIoU@inner.} Following CBL's own evaluation
protocol~\cite{tang2022contrastive}, we reuse only the \emph{ground-truth}
boundary set $B(y)$ from \cref{eq:boundary-set} to partition all $N$ points
into boundary and interior subsets, and compute ordinary mIoU
(\cref{eq:miou}) separately on each, restricting every $P_c$/$G_c$ to the
subset $S$ before taking the intersection-over-union:
\begin{equation}
  \text{IoU}_c(S) = \frac{|(P_c \cap S) \cap (G_c \cap S)|}{|(P_c \cap S) \cup (G_c \cap S)|}.
  \label{eq:iou-subset}
\end{equation}
Restricting $S$ to the ground-truth boundary set or its complement gives the
two reported metrics,
\begin{align}
  \text{mIoU@boundary} &= \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C} \text{IoU}_c\big(B(y)\big), \label{eq:miou-boundary} \\
  \text{mIoU@inner} &= \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C} \text{IoU}_c\big(B(y)^{\complement}\big), \label{eq:miou-inner}
\end{align}
directly exposing whether errors concentrate at boundaries rather than being
averaged away by interior accuracy, which dominates plain mIoU since interior
points vastly outnumber boundary points on a typical scan.

\emph{segm\_loss.} The mean per-point cross-entropy of the segmentation head
on the eval split,
\begin{equation}
  \text{segm\_loss} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \log \frac{\exp(z_{i, y_i})}{\sum_{c=1}^{C} \exp(z_{i,c})},
  \label{eq:segloss}
\end{equation}
where $z_i$ are the decoder's pre-softmax logits for point $i$, reported as a
lower-is-better sanity check alongside the four accuracy metrics above.

\textbf{Statistical convention.} Every metric is reported as mean $\pm$ raw
standard deviation across the 3 seeds; at $n{=}3$ we do not attempt a
standard-error or confidence-interval treatment. Comparisons between
ablations, and between the two codebook-init regimes, are always paired: we
compare matching \emph{seed\_index} runs (verified against each run's own
saved training seed, not merely timestamp order), so seed-to-seed variance is
a within-pair quantity rather than a between-group one, and we report the
paired mean and paired standard deviation of the difference rather than
combining the two groups' independent standard deviations. We flag a paired
delta as detectable only when its magnitude is at least $2\sigma$ of that
paired delta; below this threshold we report ``no detectable effect at
$n{=}3$ seeds'' rather than asserting a sign that could be seed noise. This
convention is used for every $\Delta$ column and effect estimate in this
report, including \cref{tab:headline}'s $\Delta$ B-IoU column.

The headline finding is negative, and we lead with it rather than bury it:
\textbf{no configuration in \cref{tab:headline} detectably improves aggregate
B-IoU over its own baseline, in either regime.} In the non-CLIP campaign, the
plain baseline's B-IoU ($78.37\pm0.42$) is the best value in the entire
column -- every added component, and every combination of them, sits at or
below it, down to $76.90\pm0.33$ for PTB+BRM+SegFix. In the CLIP campaign the
best column value (+PTB, $78.29\pm0.38$) is only $0.82$pp above the CLIP
baseline ($77.47\pm0.73$), well within the two rows' own standard deviations.
If B-IoU is the metric these methods were built to move, on this evidence
none of them move it. This is precisely why \cref{sec:per-class} exists: not
as an afterthought to a positive aggregate result, but as the reason to keep
looking once the aggregate result turns up empty.

A second pattern qualifies, without reversing, the first. mIoU@inner rises
monotonically as PTB and then BRM are added, in both regimes:
$98.80\pm0.18 \to 98.91\pm0.13 \to 99.05\pm0.14$ from baseline to +PTB to
+PTB+BRM in the non-CLIP campaign, and $98.83\pm0.24 \to 98.99\pm0.09 \to
99.04\pm0.03$ over the same chain under CLIP init. This is not a
boundary-for-interior trade-off -- mIoU@boundary over the identical non-CLIP
chain is flat-to-noisy ($90.16 \to 89.98 \to 90.17$), not falling -- so the
honest description is that boundary supervision \emph{gains interior
accuracy while holding boundary accuracy}, a gain the aggregate B-IoU metric
above is simply not built to see.

\section{Where CLIP Helps and Hurts}
\label{sec:clip-results}

\Cref{tab:headline}'s $\Delta$ B-IoU column already shows one detectable
paired effect (CLIP baseline vs.\ non-CLIP baseline) and nine null ones. This
section checks every other metric the same way -- same paired seeds, same
$2\sigma$ threshold (\cref{sec:setup}) -- rather than reading claims off raw
best-per-column cells, which can compare different configs across the two
campaign blocks and were never paired-tested.

\begin{table*}[t]
\centering
\resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{\input{table_clip_paired.tex}}
\caption{Paired (CLIP $-$ non-CLIP) delta, all 6 metrics, all 10 configs.
Bold = clears the $2\sigma$ detectability threshold; every other cell is
noise at $n{=}3$ seeds.}
\label{tab:clip-paired}

Of the 60 cells in \cref{tab:clip-paired}, exactly three are detectable.
CLIP-init on the plain baseline detectably \emph{hurts} two metrics, not one:
B-IoU ($-0.90\pm0.37$) and freq\_IoU ($-0.18\pm0.03$). Every one of the nine
non-baseline configurations neutralises both -- no config shows a detectable
B-IoU or freq\_IoU difference between its CLIP and non-CLIP version. The
third detectable cell is the only positive finding: CLIP+PTB reaches a
detectably lower segm\_loss than non-CLIP+PTB ($-0.0038\pm0.0014$), with no
accompanying detectable change on mIoU, mIoU@boundary, or mIoU@inner for that
same row -- a training-signal effect, not an evaluation-metric one, and we
report it as such rather than as ``CLIP+PTB wins.''

Read together: CLIP reshapes the codebook's class-level semantics, PTB/BRM
reshape boundary geometry through direct supervision, and on this evidence
the two barely interact -- our methods erase CLIP-init's initial damage, but
neither side measurably lifts the other beyond that one loss-level effect.

\section{Where the Signal Lives: Per-Class}
\label{sec:per-class}

Aggregate metrics average over 18 classes of very unequal difficulty; this
section reopens that average, non-CLIP (left) and CLIP-init (right)
throughout, and is the direct explanation for \cref{sec:results}'s null
result, not a contradiction of it.

\Cref{fig:per-class-heatmap} first establishes that difficulty is a property
of the class, not the config: Dress, Jumpsuits, and Scarf are the three
hardest classes under every one of the four configs, in both campaigns --
adding CBL, PTB, or BRM changes individual cells by a point or two, but never
changes which classes are hard. Nothing in this campaign closes that gap; it
only asks whether the added components move the needle within it.

Two things stand out. First, the classes that move most under PTB+BRM+CBL

effect concentrating where boundaries are actually ambiguous, rather than a
diffuse improvement spread evenly across all 18 classes. Second, Scarf
breaks that pattern: it is the third tail class by baseline difficulty but
moves slightly \emph{negative}, and this is also where the CLIP campaign
diverges most -- CLIP's largest mover is Coat, a class that was not
particularly hard to begin with. The geometric route (PTB+BRM+CBL) and the
semantic route (CLIP-init) are evidently picking up different signal, not
the same signal through two mechanisms.

The mean bars in \cref{fig:per-class-delta} hide exactly the distinction that
matters here. Dress's gain holds up at the seed level: 2 of 3 seeds move up,
consistent with a real, if not universal, effect. Jumpsuits' mean gain is
not: its three seeds disagree on \emph{direction}, so the positive mean is an
artefact of averaging, not a signal any individual training run actually
shows. Scarf barely moves at either level of aggregation. Of the three
classes we picked out as the largest movers, only one (Dress) survives
looking at individual seeds rather than their mean -- which is precisely the
honesty this figure is for.

\Cref{fig:variance-scatter} is the ceiling story that anchors this whole
section. Ten of the eighteen classes already sit at $\geq 0.96$ baseline
IoU, in a tight cluster at the bottom-right of the plot: little headroom left
to gain, and correspondingly little seed-to-seed variance to fight through.
Almost all of the room any method has to show an effect -- and almost all of
the seed noise standing in the way of detecting one -- is concentrated in a
handful of classes at the other end (Dress, Jumpsuits, Scarf, Body, Hair,
Shoes). This is why \cref{sec:results}'s aggregate B-IoU stays flat: it
averages 18 classes, ten of which have almost nothing left to move, diluting
whatever the tail classes do into noise. It is also why \cref{fig:tail-closeup}'s
seed-level disagreement on Jumpsuits is not a fluke -- the tail classes carry
the most real signal and the most real noise simultaneously, by construction.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Put together, the three results sections tell one consistent story rather
than three separate ones. \Cref{sec:results} found that no configuration
detectably improves aggregate B-IoU over baseline, in either codebook-init
regime. \Cref{sec:per-class} explains why: ten of eighteen classes are
already at or above 0.96 baseline IoU, with essentially no headroom and no
seed variance left, so any real per-class effect is diluted across mostly-
saturated classes the moment it is averaged into a single number. The effect
is there -- Dress moves up under PTB+BRM+CBL in 2 of 3 seeds, a real if not
universal gain -- but Jumpsuits' apparent gain turns out to be two seeds
disagreeing on direction averaging to a positive mean, and Scarf barely
moves at all. \Cref{sec:results:tradeoff} adds that where boundary
supervision does show an effect, it shows up as \emph{gained} interior
accuracy with boundary accuracy held rather than traded away -- not the
failure mode we went in expecting, but not the boundary win the methods were
built to produce either.

\Cref{sec:clip-results}'s finding composes with this rather than
complicating it. CLIP-init detectably damages the plain baseline on two
metrics (B-IoU and freq\_IoU), and every one of our nine other configurations
neutralises both -- but only neutralises them. The one place CLIP leaves a
positive, detectable trace anywhere in the 60-run campaign is a lower
training loss under PTB specifically, with no accompanying detectable
accuracy gain. Read plainly: the geometric route (PTB/BRM/CBL) and the
semantic route (CLIP-init) each do something real, but on this evidence they
do not add up to more than either does alone, and \cref{sec:per-class}'s
finding that CLIP's largest per-class mover (Coat) is not even the same
class as PTB+BRM+CBL's (Dress, Jumpsuits) suggests they are not picking up
the same signal in the first place. \Cref{sec:results:segfix}'s SegFix
finding fits the same pattern: a suggestive but non-detectable drop,
plausibly a hyperparameter-scale artefact rather than a mechanism failure.

\textbf{Limitations.} We name four explicitly, all with the evidence behind
them already in this report rather than asserted after the fact.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{$n=3$ is the binding constraint on almost every null result in
        this paper.} Tail-class seed spread is roughly $1.5$--$2.2$pp on
        Dress and Jumpsuits (\cref{fig:tail-closeup}) -- large enough that a
        real effect of that size cannot clear our $2\sigma$ threshold at
        this seed count, which is exactly the situation Jumpsuits appears to
        be in. More seeds, not a different method, is the direct way to
        resolve this.
  \item \emph{Single test set.} Every number in this report is on
        CloSe-D augmented with THuman2.0 (\cref{sec:methodology:thuman}); we
        did not evaluate on GarmentCode or any other out-of-distribution set
        as originally scoped, and did not separately evaluate a
        THuman2.0-free baseline, so we cannot isolate the augmentation's own
        effect from the methods it was meant to support.
  \item \emph{SegFix's hyperparameters were not tuned for this domain.} Its
        threshold and step size are inherited defaults, not swept for
        unit-bounding-box point clouds, and \cref{sec:results:segfix}'s
        suggestive drop is consistent with (though not proof of) that
        mismatch.
  \item \emph{The qualitative comparison is deferred, not abandoned.} A
        per-point, colour-coded baseline-vs-PTB+BRM+CBL comparison on real
        scans needs the trained checkpoints, which live on the training
        cluster rather than in this local environment; we plan to add it
        once that access is available.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We integrated PTB-style auxiliary supervision, a re-homed refinement module
(BRM), point-cloud SegFix, contrastive boundary learning, and a
CLIP-initialised codebook into CloSe-Net, and evaluated all ten combinations
under both a random and a CLIP-initialised codebook, three seeds each. The
headline result is negative and we report it as such: none of these methods
detectably improves aggregate B-IoU over the plain baseline, in either
regime. That result does not mean nothing happened. Per-class analysis
shows a real, seed-consistent gain on at least one hard class (Dress) that
the aggregate metric cannot see because ten of eighteen classes are already
saturated; the boundary-vs-interior scatter shows the methods gain interior
accuracy without losing boundary accuracy; and the CLIP campaign shows a
specific, detectable, and previously unquantified interaction -- CLIP-init
damages the plain baseline, our methods neutralise that damage, and the
methods and CLIP-init otherwise leave each other alone. The overall
picture is a method that moves real but narrow, class-concentrated signal
that a single aggregate number is not built to expose -- and a set of tools
(paired seed-matched testing, per-class breakdowns, honest per-seed plots)
for finding that signal rather than either missing it or overclaiming it.

Future work follows directly from the limitations above: a properly-seeded
CBL feature-stage ablation (decoder vs.\ encoder-stage vs.\ multi-scale,
\cref{sec:methodology:cbl}); a point-cloud-scale hyperparameter sweep for
SegFix; the deferred qualitative visualisation once training-cluster
checkpoint access is available; and evaluation on an out-of-distribution set
such as GarmentCode, which was in the original scope of this project and
would test whether the per-class pattern found here (real signal on hard,
boundary-heavy classes; little movement on easy ones) generalises beyond
CloSe-D and THuman2.0.
